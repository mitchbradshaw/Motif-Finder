% Block card: detection.noise_threshold -- "Noise threshold" (Part I, the blocks chapter).
\cardfield{What it does}
Marks every stretch where the signal strays further from its usual level than a chosen
number of times its own background noise.

\cardfield{When to use it}
As a first, simple detector that works the same on every channel, whatever its size and
whether it is stored in volts or millivolts: you never type a level, only ``how many times the
noise''. Put \blockref{preprocessing.detrend} in front; the ready-made template
\texttt{noise\_threshold} does, and looks for drops 4 noise widths below the usual level.

\begin{plainterms}[The noise width]
To decide what counts as unusual, the block first measures how much the signal normally
wobbles. It does not use the ordinary spread, which the events themselves would inflate.
Instead it takes the typical distance of a sample from the middle value, which a few large
events barely move: like judging the usual height of waves on a lake by the middle wave,
not the average, so that one passing boat does not change the answer.
\end{plainterms}

\cardfield{What goes in, what comes out}
In goes a stretch of one channel. Out come the stretches that cross the cut, each scored by
how many noise widths past the cut it reaches.

\cardfield{Settings that matter}
\emph{k\_sigma}: the cut, in noise widths. 4 is the value from the spike-sorting paper it
comes from; lower finds weaker events and more false ones. \emph{direction}: \texttt{below} for
drops, \texttt{above} for rises, \texttt{either} (the default) for both.
\emph{noise\_from}: \texttt{rolling} measures the noise over a moving window instead of the
whole stretch, for a channel whose noise changes over days.

\cardfield{Watch out for}
The noise width is only honest when events are a small part of the trace. When they fill much
of it, they inflate the noise width and hide under it; the block warns when more than a fifth of
the samples are past the cut, and prints a second number to compare when even that check is
blind.
