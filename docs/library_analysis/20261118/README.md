# Library shape map and sequence trends: first prototype (18 Nov 2026)

Script: `scripts/library_analysis/shape_map_sequences_20261118.py` (read-only, ~30 s). Inputs: the 9 Nov accepted
catalogue (`docs/thesis/data/accepted_catalogue_20261109/`) and its Chapter 9 measurements
(`docs/thesis/data/ch9_inventory_20261109/measures.csv`). Those measurements come from the tool's own event-shape block
(`Working/interrogation/event_shape.py`). Every number below is regenerated by the script.

## In plain words

Think of the library as a box of photographs. Each photo is a stretch of signal that a person said "yes, that's
interesting" to. The 2,959 figure counts **photos, not events**. One photo can show one drop, several drops in a row,
or a whole hour of activity. So before plotting events we sort the photos: one drop, several drops, or too long.

Then we place each single drop on a map. Left to right is how long it takes to fall and climb halfway back (its
width). Bottom to top is how far it falls (its depth). Next we cut the map into a 5 × 5 grid. In each square we draw
the most typical real drop there. We colour that drop blue wherever the square's drops agree in shape and red
wherever they disagree.

For sequences, we line up drops that follow each other on the same channel. Then we ask whether they get deeper,
wider or more frequent over time, like checking whether the waves get bigger as the tide comes in.

## 2. Individual events: the shape map

- `shape_map_scatter.png`: every usable single event, width against depth, on log axes. The dashed lines are the
  grid. Hollow triangles are drops that had not half-recovered when the search stopped. They are drawn at their
  **lower bound**, so they are really further right.
- `shape_map_by_recording.png`: the same plane, one recording at a time.
- `shape_map_medoid_grid.png`: the 5 × 5 medoid grid. Each waveform is aligned at the onset of its fall. The colour is
  the **spread of shape** across the square's events: each event is divided by its own depth first, so the colour
  shows differences in form and timing, not leftover differences in size. Spread is 0 at the onset by construction.

What the grid shows. The per-phase means are in `grid_cells.csv`.

- **The fall is the most consistent part everywhere.** Spread during the fall is 0.14–0.35 in every populated square.
- **Wide events disagree on the way back up, not on the way down.** For the two widest columns, recovery spread is
  0.5–1.4.
- **Your "different approaches" idea holds, but only for wide, shallow events.** Spread before the onset is 0.43–0.68
  there. Deep events (over 42 mV) start from a flat baseline, with spread 0.02–0.05.
- **The narrow mid-depth squares are one shape.** They are dominated by MJu26a CH14 (326 of 339 in the busiest
  square). The shape is a square-bottomed drop with a plateau and an abrupt recovery, and it is blue end to end.
- **The narrow, deep corner is almost empty.** Only one accepted event deeper than 13 mV has a width under 5 s.
- The curved "strings" of points around 5–30 s are not duplicates (no two onsets lie within 5 s). They are CH14's
  events drifting deeper over the 90 h train.
- Most triangles (502 of 849) are M2_aug and LM drops that do not climb halfway back within 10 fall-widths. These are
  step-like drops, and the width measure cannot place them yet.

## 3. Sequences: how shape changes along a run

- `sequence_trend_summary.png`: Kendall's tau of each measure against time, one dot per sequence.
- `sequence_trends.png`: the headline figure, **depth and interval**. Columns: as measured (44 sequences); the longest
  sequence on its own; normalised (value ÷ the sequence's median, time ÷ its duration).
- `sequence_trends_all_measures.png`: the same layout for all five measures (depth, fall time, width, fall rate,
  interval).

**Across sequences there is no shared direction.** Each measure trends (p < 0.05) in 3–8 sequences, against about 2
expected by chance. But the significant trends split up and down, and the median tau is about 0 for all five. So
individual sequences change, but not in one common way.

**The 90 h CH14 train (S01, 525 events) changes strongly:**

| Measure | Kendall's tau | Last third ÷ first third |
|---|---:|---:|
| Depth | +0.49 | 1.55 |
| Fall time | −0.30 | 0.86 |
| Fall rate | +0.56 | 1.78 |
| Interval | −0.28 | 0.50 |

The 8 Nov CH14 case (6.6 / 9.1 / 10.1 mV by thirds) agrees: the events get deeper, fall faster and come about twice as
often.

**A pattern worth checking by eye (`sequence_width_alternation.csv`).** In CH14, "wide" events (more than twice the
median width; about 20 s against 6 s) almost never come back to back: 2 such pairs in 468 events. Neighbours switch
class 49 % of the time, against 37 % when the order is shuffled (p ≈ 0.0002). Wide events are also deeper (11.9 against
7.6 mV) and follow shorter gaps (438 against 727 s). This could be real alternation, like a heart's alternans, or it
could be a measurement effect of a drop arriving before the last one has recovered. Look at a run of them before
believing it.

## Caveats

- **The block measured the wrong thing in 411 spans (14 %), mostly MJu26a (191).** The event-shape anatomy starts from
  the steepest single sample. In a several-minute 10 Hz span, that sample can be a noise wiggle, which produces a
  "0.1 mV, 0.5 s event" inside a 10 mV drop. A random check of 12 flagged spans showed the markers off the real fall in
  every one. Such spans are flagged when the measured depth is under 30 % of the span's own range (after a 1 s average)
  and are left out of both parts. This is why the CH15 long train nearly disappears from the sequences: 84 of its 143
  members are flagged, and 21 are over an hour. Several of its machine-proposed windows also start after the fall has
  begun.
- **Intervals between accepted events reflect what was reviewed, not only what happened.** For example, if deeper
  events were easier to propose, CH14's shrinking interval could partly be detection. The 1 h join rule also caps every
  interval at 1 h.
- Multi-fall spans join chains by their main fall. Their other falls are not yet members.
- The width needs half recovery within 10 fall-widths (the block's default). That is the same as the thesis numbers,
  and it is why 849 events are bounds rather than values.

## Why the event-shape block was not changed (18 Nov, later)

I tried to fix the mis-measurement in `Working/interrogation/event_shape.py` and **reverted it**. The tree is as it
was; nothing was committed. The finding is that this is a question of definition, not a bug.

The block's anatomy measures the **steep core** of a fall. It takes the steepest point, then stops at the "knee", the
first point where the slope has eased to 5 % of that steepest point. That is the detector's own framing ("the most
vertical drop at the start of the drop"). It is right for detector events: on the 3,511 `drop_motifs10` events, it
lands within 3 samples of the detector's own trough 93 % of the time.

On human-accepted spans the steep core often isn't what the reviewer saw. Of the 12+16 flagged spans checked by eye,
the failures were:

- 10 Hz sample noise making the "steepest point" a noise sample;
- exponential-shaped decays that are steepest at their very start;
- notched falls, where a small late step is the steepest point;
- windows that begin after the fall has started.

Every change tried trades one population against the other:

| Rule | Accepted spans flagged (of 2,914) | Detector troughs within 1 s, 10 Hz | Notes |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Current (steep core, raw) | 411 | 99.6 % | |
| Deepest fall, then the same rules | 393 | 85 % | barely helps; onsets move on 454 already-good spans |
| Deepest fall, knee on a smoothed copy | 208–237 | ~73–85 % | fixes 10 Hz (191 → 27); 1–2 Hz untouched |
| Steep core on a smoothed copy | n/a | 98 % | depth agreement with the detector falls from 75 % to 54 % |
| **Whole drop**, top to bottom of the deepest fall | **31** | 85 % | a different measure: fall time ×2.9 (median), depth moves >10 % on half the spans |

So the block stays as it is: it is correct for what it was built to measure. What the shape map should use for
human-accepted spans is the researcher's choice. Your plot's wording, "height from start of drop to trough", describes
the whole drop.
